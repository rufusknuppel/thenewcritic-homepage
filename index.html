MrBeast, Slop Auteur

“According to the memo, MrBeast’s art justifies all — there is no reason to live, it seems, except to make a MrBeast video.”

Sep 16

The Art of YouTube

The Art of YouTube

Would you do what it takes for wealth and fame? Would you humiliate, debase, and torture yourself for a shot at stardom? There is a man who has done what it takes to become rich and famous, and he now tirelessly uses his wealth and fame to put other people in humiliating, debasing, and torturous situations for their own opportunities at success. Before that, though, he was a nobody with a dream. His dream was YouTube fame, and the only way he could achieve this dream was through understanding its algorithm.

Raised an evangelical by a single mother in Greenville, North Carolina, he was an introvert with consuming obsessions. As a youth, his obsessions were split between YouTube and baseball. He even hoped to become a MLB player. But this idea was crushed when he was diagnosed at the age of 15 with Crohn’s disease. The strain this condition put on his body forced him to give up on baseball and retreat indoors. He saw potential in online video creators who were starting to make real money in brand deals: YouTube would be his salvation.

He soon joined a group of other minor YouTubers who spent 10 hours a day studying how to go viral. He learned how to make the optimal title and thumbnail, along with hundreds of other minute aspects of editing, lighting, and pacing that gain and maintain attention. But despite his efforts, the algorithm was cruel. By the age of 19, after seven years of toil, he had been lucky enough to make a few dollars. He ceased to care about money, or art, or even love. He cared only about YouTube.

But after an entire youth wasted, after being unable to speak to kids at school about anything other than YouTube — and even then, his obsession for the topic often drove everyone away from discussing it with him — after dropping out of community college and being kicked out of his home by a mother who knew in her heart that her son was ruining his God-given life by spending it on a website dominated by children and gamers, the young man was finally about to become somebody. Grimacing, he sat in his chair, turned on the camera, and spoke.

“I’m about to count to 100,000. If you don’t believe me, just watch this video. All I want to say is, just Tweet the video out, share it with maybe Keem or Scarce, and if you’re a YouTuber, just help a guy out. 1 Think of the pain I’m about to go through — screw it. Let’s just — let’s. One.”

As he started counting, did he know? So mastered by the brute power of the algorithm, was he aware of what was to come? Did he see the hordes of people chanting his name, the cities he would build? Allegedly, he only remembers saying “one” — the remaining 999,999 numbers passed by like a bad dream. Regardless of whatever he dreamed after inflicting 40 hours of torture on himself, the young man said 100,000 and collapsed into his chair. Born Jimmy Donaldson in 1997, he had, with this act, officially crowned himself before the world with his true title: MrBeast.

Jimmy Donaldson uploaded “I Counted To 100,000!” on January 9, 2017. As of 2026, he is a billionaire. His main MrBeast YouTube channel has over 500 million subscribers, representing 6% of the world population. This account is the most subscribed-to YouTube channel in the world, and this is not counting MrBeast Gaming, Beast Philanthropy, Beast Reacts, MrBeast 2, and Beast Animations. His reality TV show, Beast Games, is Amazon’s most viewed unscripted show — Beast Games had the most contestants in reality television history (1,000) and the biggest cash-prize ($5,000,000). He’s a philanthropist who has dug wells, healed the blind, and planted trees; he has spent seven days buried alive in a coffin, seven days in solitary confinement, and seven days in a cave. He’ll do anything for content, damnit.

After counting to 100,000, MrBeast received a brand deal for $10,000 from a now-defunct NFT marketplace. He was 19 years old; he could have bought nice clothes, or invested the money, or thrown a big party — which is probably what I would have done. He could have bought better camera equipment — which is what any other self-serious YouTube grinder-entrepreneur probably would have done. Instead, MrBeast did something truly savvy. He stuffed all $10,000 in an envelope, gave it to a panhandler, and filmed it the whole time — that’s late-capitalist Jesus Christ right there! The shifty freak has a supernatural ability to sniff out content. He’ll do something absurd and attention-grabbing, and then, once he has the attention of millions, advertisers will flock to pay him; finally, with the money from advertising, he’ll fund another striking exploit. By constantly trapping the viewer in an incessant, hyperactive, compulsive cycle, the MrBeast machine — get attention, get investments, pump that into the next scheme, and constantly refine, reinvest, and compete — hurtles forward.

Watching a MrBeast video, though, you, too, start to get hungry; you want to compete. Though his challenges require no skill, or capital, or even intelligence — only determination and a willingness to humiliate oneself — you are compelled to ask yourself, What would I do for this money, this fame, this salvation? Could I stand in a circle for hours, or spend months of my life without leaving a grocery store, or count to 100,000 nonstop?

MrBeast’s competitions — like his real life “Squid Game” with 456 contestants, a $456,000 prize, and an accurate reproduction of the Korean Netflix show, minus the killing — are massive productions. But his challenges, in which people dare to withstand absurd situations like living in a grocery store, spending 100 days in a red circle, or spending 100 days in a private jet, for monetary rewards, tend to be his most ridiculous videos. “Last To Leave Grocery Store, Wins $250,000” is emblematic of this category. In it, the Beast buys a grocery store in his hometown of Greenville, North Carolina and announces a 50%-off sale, thus enticing a discount-hungry crowd into the store. Once it is full, MrBeast announces over a megaphone that the last person to leave the grocery store will win $250,000 — they can use anything in the grocery store to their advantage, but they can’t leave.

Jasmine Sun’s Project of Self-Transformation

On Sontag, Stanford, and Silicon Valley

Aug 19

Journalistic Becoming

Journalistic Becoming

Jasmine Sun is the author of the popular Substack “Jasmine’s Substack.” She is a contributing writer to The Atlantic, has published multiple pieces in The New York Times, and was recently interviewed by Ezra Klein. Her beat is the technologies and social mores of Silicon Valley. In her writing about Chinese peptides, the “permanent underclass,” and the furor over the construction of data centers, she has become the go-to “anthropologist of disruption.” She is 26.

“I’m only interested in people engaged in a project of self-transformation,” Susan Sontag wrote in her journals. Jasmine connected with that quote immediately upon reading it: she has been engaged in that project for a long-time. She joined the 826 writing seminars at 8-years-old, and was a champion high-school debater. At Stanford, she realized that the start-up world was a matter more of knowing the language than anything else, so she read the Silicon Valley canon. When she couldn’t get a job as a journalist, she started work at Substack, eventually becoming a project manager leading the core product team, before resigning to write full-time.

In her advice to college graduates of the class of 2026, Jasmine writes: “The old world was bad. Those staid institutions and rigid hierarchies are not worth mourning.” We can no longer expect that going to the right schools and checking the right boxes guarantees a life of wealth and flourishing; that world is gone. In this world where Substack, San Francisco, and AI all determine our future, we all have something to learn from Jasmine Sun.

I, unfortunately, did a lot of competitive debate growing up, so this is probably a big part of why I feel reasonably comfortable speaking on podcasts. I spent a lot of time doing the kind of debate in high school where you talk about critical theory at 300 words per minute. I feel like everybody in that community senses an affinity with others — I can name all of the other writers who used to be policy debaters off the top of my head. Ben Lerner did Policy, Sally Rooney did British Parliamentary, Nate Silver was a Policy debater, Jay Caspian Kang was a Policy debater, Kelsey Piper was a Policy debater, and Jerusalem Demsas debated, too.

That probably helped a lot with getting good at making arguments orally without having everything written down and thought-through beforehand — it’s bullshitting, frankly. You just learn to be really good at bullshitting when you’re 14 years old. One skill that I’m unusually good at relative to other people, which is not a very interesting superpower, is cross-examination. I love being asked hard questions. Before I was a writer, I worked at Substack, and I loved doing town hall events where everyone was angry and asking, “Why are you ruining my app? Why would you introduce video to Substack? Why would you introduce a social feed?” It was super fun to think about hard questions and try to get good answers.

I always really enjoyed being cross-examined, and a podcast is just a very long cross-examination.

One of the things that makes people incredibly charismatic, whether you’re talking about Susan Sontag, or Bernie Sanders, or Steve Jobs, is they might be assholes — they might not even be that smart or that correct a lot of the time — but charisma comes, in part, purely from the sense that this person is acting in total accordance with their will — there is no separate inner and outer self. And I think that Sontag, and Steve Jobs, and, you know, our most charismatic politicians and business leaders, do embody that. I don’t think charisma is the only thing that matters — I don’t think charisma is the same as virtue — but I do find that really interesting.

People on the East Coast in media love to ask me, “Why are they talking about killing everybody with AI? Why are you trying to sell your product by saying it’s gonna take everybody’s job and go rogue and commit a cybercrime,” or whatever? Part of this is that Dario Amodei really, really believes that AI might cause permanent runaway inequality, and also that it might cause a bioterrorist attack that kills several million people. And I think that there is charisma that attracts a lot of these “autistic” AI researchers to working with Dario, because this guy has a unity of conscience and will and action. He is saying exactly the thing that is within him. It’s not charismatic in the sense of sort of a normal, likable, I-would-grab-a-beer-with-him thing. It’s kind of the opposite of that. But the charisma comes from this sense that he is fully, authentically himself, and he’s that committed to his cause. But then it causes problems, of course.

Sam Altman, for example, for a long time was considered incredibly, incredibly charismatic. That’s how he was always able to fail upward: from his failed start-up, to being president of YC, and then kind of fired from YC, to OpenAI, to being fired from OpenAI, to coming back even more powerful than ever. It’s like, how does this guy keep failing up? And it’s because people in tech and AI consider him very charismatic and very relationally competent, in a way that certainly none of the rest of the world thinks of Sam Altman. He does, though, have a reality distortion field around him in the same way, so that when he talks about AI, it is imbued with a sense of existential weight, and people really sense that he believes that the future could be that different, and that great, or that awe-inspiring. People just sense that in Sam Altman’s future, the world could look very different. When they’re around him, they get excited by that. I don’t think that excites a lot of people, but it excites people in the AI world.

It would be really great if, by 2030, I felt like I was, in my head, the best tech journalist. It’s unclear what this means. It’s totally undefined. I need to win tech journalism by 2030, and then I will switch careers. And I don’t know what I’ll do. My assumption is just that if I win, I will be bored, and then I will want to do something else. Maybe I’ll get really into writing fiction. I’ve never really written fiction since I was a kid. Maybe I’ll go back into the tech industry. Maybe I’ll be really interested in philanthropy. I have no idea. I don’t really care. It’s not that deep.

Aug 12

Law School Elites

Law School Elites

I’m worried nostalgia is a fascist emotion, I once announced to my law school housemate, a man who was forcefully subjected to my internal monologue for over two years. Gideon would consider my claim while reheating his leftovers. So is hunger, he’d rejoin, gesturing at the microwave. Then I would go upstairs and be nostalgic anyway.

I recently graduated from one of the top law schools in the United States. While I was there, I became terribly nostalgic for my childhood. It wasn’t because I had a good childhood. I grew up in a troubled household in New Zealand. Neither of my parents went to college; my grandparents are illiterate. We were constantly in debt, and everyone in my family blamed each other for it. Nothing about my upbringing involved unusual disadvantage, but I don’t think I met anyone else at my law school whose parents owed them money. People like Gideon were far more common there; his grandfather was an alum, and he had parents in academia. So my nostalgia puzzled me at first, because I was deeply unhappy in New Zealand. But after law school, I realized I missed reading the phrase “making the world a better place” without experiencing its mandatory sarcastic aftertaste. I missed not knowing how power really worked.

A selective law school is simultaneously serious and unserious. It’s a trade school, but it’s also a finishing school. In the United States, it is often a pipeline to the political class, so it has to be taken seriously as a culture; simultaneously, non-interdisciplinary legal academia is a joke, and law reviews may be the most embarrassing form of knowledge production on the planet. When you enter a selective law school, the world you come to reside in becomes extraordinarily parochial. I attended an elite institution as an undergraduate, too, but even a college with billionaires still has thousands of students majoring in diverse fields — the population is large enough to disappear into. Elite law schools permit no such disappearance. Mine had the class size of a high school and the claustrophobic social dynamics to match.

Because everyone is entering adjacent rungs of the same profession, there is the ugly sense that everyone might know everyone, forever. When I intern at an organization across the country, middle-aged staff attorneys ask me how my professors are doing. When I visit one friend at Oxford, another friend’s ex-boyfriend is in the same PhD cohort. When I go to dinner with anyone from Princeton, they jokingly threaten to FaceTime my failed talking stage who went there, too. And because this world is so insular, people behave in ways that are extraordinarily repellent to any normal person. Everyone is reasonably smart — they write solid papers that make incremental claims, or they’re Rhodes Scholars. They also have a pathetic need for validation that comes from being too good too young, and most will spend their entire lives reinforcing the status quo.

There was a game that I largely didn’t play but was acutely aware of as a law student: secret dinners with Supreme Court justices, six-figure book deals, penthouse parties, private island visits, appellate clerkships secretly offered in the spring semester of our first year. Because I was foreign, and therefore politically irrelevant, I could watch this unfold as one watches reality TV. My ongoing “bit” with Gideon was that of a well-meaning but bigoted anthropologist dispatched from my country to record the customs of the American ruling class. Certain professors and law students ought to be brought back to my civilization, I suggested, to be exhibited. Because everyone was competitive, ludicrously granular kinds of hierarchy emerged. One professor taught a seminar on nobility, advocating directly for the “aristocratic” notion that some human beings are superior at the art of living. Several professors refused to hire research assistants who hadn’t attended Harvard, Yale, Princeton, Stanford or MIT (HYPSM) as undergraduates. One professor was banned from partaking in the admissions process altogether because he would simply rate any applicant who hadn’t gone to Harvard a 0.

Every time I returned to New Zealand, I found it increasingly difficult to explain what law school was like. My mother might ask me what classes I was taking this semester, and I might say that I was taking Federal Courts. But I wasn’t taking Federal Courts. I was bearing witness to Biblical hubris. The class was taught by an infamous professor perpetually dressed in a pooling suit; I often joked that he needed to be gently vacuumed. He would compare himself to Jesus, without being Kanye. He spent most of his lectures seething about how his brother had clerked for the Supreme Court instead of him. He would name-drop past teaching assistants of his who had ascended to the Solicitor General’s office, he would applaud himself for not having a chauffeur. He would refer to lower court judges as “automata.” And for ideological reasons, this professor refused to teach habeas (a petition used to challenge the legality of detention), advocating that all we needed to know is that we should deny them when we clerked. The class collectively assembled a “bingo card” for these pet topics.

This is torture for someone as bitchily irrepressible as I am. You can’t laugh, because these people run America. You have to laugh, because these people are flagrantly ridiculous. Many of my classmates and professors did not regularly interact with people outside their social class; their brains cannot proportion risk. They agonize over the prestige gap between Wachtell and Simpson Thacher, two corporate firms whose differences are only perceptible from inside the terrarium. They scoff at the idea of clerking on the Fourth Circuit, a job most law graduates in the country might light a candle for.

Just as I saw the social ladder as a kind of ethnography, my other classmates saw it as a winnable game. And it is winnable, that is the unspeakable thrill of it. You can go to this law school, and later become the president, or a Supreme Court justice — many people have done this before. But opportunities for ascension are not transparently awarded. If your grandparents were alumni, you arrived knowing this — I had to make sad eyes at upperclassmen until they relented and told me. Only some professors could offer you Power, and they didn’t offer you Power in exchange for work. You were either anointed with love, or you weren’t. One professor explicitly compared the legal profession’s client-patronage system to racehorse pedigrees; he was mentored by Owen Fiss, a famous constitutional law scholar, so his fursona (he wouldn’t call it that) was Seabiscuit. He referred to his favorite students as the future Abraham Lincolns and Frederick Douglasses.

But once you acquire anything on the basis of nepotism, you can lose these things on the basis of interpersonal meddling, too; my classmates viciously doxxed each other. When conservatives snitched on others they deemed insufficiently Catholic, clerkships were rescinded, and jobs were lost. The result was an eerie social environment organized around ostentatious de-risking. Decades in advance, people were fanatically pruning away behavior that might complicate a confirmation hearing. Some of my classmates would not dance or drink at socials. Some carried burner phones abroad, they were so paranoid about surveillance.

My friend Adam was the gentlest case. In our second year, I texted him a question I considered weightless: was Professor R. a good lecturer, or should I take “Administrative Law” with someone else, instead? The typing bubble surfaced and vanished several times. Then he asked, Are you alone? I was alone, and my phone rang. “I’m sorry, I don’t want to put that kind of thing in writing,” he told me . Put what in writing? “My opinion,” he’d added with the patience of someone explaining fire to a child. Adam may want to be a judge one day.

Present at the Creation

On documenting the Singularity

Aug 10

Monitoring the Situation

Monitoring the Situation

Two years ago, the chief-of-staff to Anthropic’s CEO declared, “These next five years might be the last few years that I work.” At the time, she spoke for a world that knows far more about the future than the average American. But even before the release of ChatGPT, Theo Jaffee knew that the future was happening in the West. The 21-year-old University of Florida drop-out found his computer science classes in Gainesville pointless; they operated under old assumptions about how to build a career. Come summertime, he left for San Francisco, drawing on connections with former Curtis Yarvin intern Stevie Miller, attending the Manifest conference in Berkeley, and building out his Twitter following. Now Jaffee is the co-founder of Monitoring The Situation (MTS), an Andreesen-Horowitz backed media company that streams interviews of central players in AI and technology all day on X (formerly Twitter). Jaffee is San Francisco’s go-to reporter and AGI discussant, tirelessly cataloguing, describing, and perhaps influencing the future of our world.

A Spanish ruler declared long ago, “Had I been present at the Creation, I would have given some useful hints for the better ordering of the universe.” But if you cannot be present at the creation — well, at least you can monitor the situation. Below, I talk to Jaffee about becoming “AGI-pilled” and what we should do to prepare for a superintelligence we’re all underestimating.

This was my freshman year of college. I didn’t have an internship for that summer, so I decided — kind of like one of these people I knew from Twitter, Dwarkesh Patel — I would start a podcast where I’d find people I liked on Twitter and interview them. This was summer of 2023, three years ago now. I started interviewing a few people. My first guest was Greg Fodor. Some of the more famous people I interviewed were Zvi Mowshowitz, Robin Hanson, Bryan Caplan, and so on. I did maybe 20 episodes over the two years of the podcast — I definitely should have done more.

In August 2023, I visited SF for the first time. I didn’t meet any mutuals then, but I discovered the city and fell in love with the place. I was like, “Wow. It’s beautiful, it’s full of technology and tech people — I definitely need to be here after college.” I don’t love the city as much anymore, but I still love the people.

Throughout my sophomore year — fall 2023 through spring 2024 — I continued being really interested in AI and AGI. This was the GPT-4 Turbo era, and then Claude 3 Opus came out around that time, too. I continued reading Yarvin and other right-wing people like Richard Hanania. That summer — May 2024 — I again didn’t have an internship. I had a few thousand dollars, and I figured if I had nothing else to do, I might as well go to SF full-time and do my podcast, hoping something would fall into place.

My Airbnb for the summer turned out to be a fake listing, so I had to stay in a hostel with shared rooms for almost three weeks until I found a more permanent place, which was the Minerva building at 16 Turk Street in the Tenderloin — it was ancient, decrepit, had no amenities, and I shared a dorm-style room with one other guy. It was $950 a month. That was summer 2024.

When I got to SF, I had about a thousand Twitter followers. I posted some good tweets in the first couple weeks that doubled me to about 2,000, and I started meeting people in real life, partly through Manifold Markets’s Taco Tuesdays, hosted by Manifold. I got to know Stephen Grugett and Austin Chen and, to a lesser extent, James Grugett. I met a lot of Twitter people, tech people, rationalists, in real life. In May, a couple weeks after I arrived, I went to a Scott Alexander meetup at Lighthaven in Berkeley. It was my first time there. I fell in love with the place. There were so many people there I knew from the internet, and the venue itself is beautiful.

That weekend was Manifest. I had discounted student tickets. It was the greatest time of my life. It was incredible to be around all these people who lived and breathed the stuff I’d been reading and writing about on Twitter. By that point, I’d been on Twitter for almost two years and had been posting for about a year.

I should mention that I heavily credit Stevie Miller, above just about anyone, for helping me get into this. We’ve known each other since we were about eight. We went to the same elementary school in Florida for a couple of years. He moved to D.C. but kept visiting because his grandmother lived in Florida. He introduced me first to Neoreaction, then to Curtis Yarvin, then to the SF rationalists. I found LessWrong independently, but he showed me it was a whole movement — told me about Manifold Taco Tuesday, told me about Manifest. I owe an immense debt to Stevie.

During summer 2024, I applied for and got an Emergent Ventures grant from Tyler Cowen’s Mercatus Center. That’s what let me survive in SF the rest of the summer. I came back to school after that, constantly thinking about going back to SF.

Manifest Man

“At Manifest, though, there is a certain existential loneliness, a specifically sexual strain of angst, that did not lift once in the three days of the conference.”

Jul 13

Rationalist Workshop

Rationalist Workshop

On a cool June evening in Berkeley, I watched a man named Victor try to shoot a beam of light out of his penis. Minutes earlier, he had raised his hand because he found the volunteer onstage before him attractive. And now here he was, facing her in front of a crowd of perhaps 60 people, having been instructed to do precisely this by a woman named Aella, a pseudonymous camgirl and self-described sex researcher. She had promised the men in the room that the exercise, if undertaken in earnest, might actually result in hookups, real ones. Now she told her first subject to imagine himself gathering all of the energy in his body into his pelvis and then sending that energy outward as a beam of light, straight through his trousers and into the room.

Victor pointed out that this seemed a little absurd, which it manifestly was. But he did it anyway, standing there and trying — and you could see him trying — to imagine himself emitting light from the tip of his penis. Aella reported that Victor wasn’t shooting enough light, and he readjusted. She and the volunteer watched. The rest of us did, too. And then the volunteer delivered her verdict, which was that Victor had come up short. She had, in fact, “felt more” from him a moment earlier, before he’d begun to imagine light streaming out of his phallus.

This was Manifest, and Victor’s ordeal belonged to its official programming. The conference — this was its fourth iteration — convenes each summer at Lighthaven, a verdant complex of gardens and halls in a converted Berkeley hotel that rationalists keep as a gathering place. At Manifest, rationalism has little to do with Descartes; it’s a Bay Area subculture built around the site LessWrong, a “community blog” preoccupied with mathematics, philosophy, genetics, and the apocalyptic risks (and opportunities) presented by artificial intelligence.

Around 800 people attend Manifest each year. It takes its name, and its nominal subject, from Manifold, a prediction market. At the opening assembly, Austin Chen, co-founder of Manifold, asked everyone who had ever bet on it to stand, and very nearly the whole audience got to its feet. Officially, then, this is a conference about forecasting. But “a conference about prediction markets” describes Manifest about as well as “a conference about effigies” would describe Burning Man. Prediction markets are what the attendees have in common, but they are not, mostly, what they came to discuss.

Any attendee could propose a session on any topic throughout the weekend, and several hundred sessions took place over the three days, a dozen or so underway in any given hour. At one session, attendees proposed political systems that might replace representative democracy (among them “futarchy,” a governance framework in which voters set overarching goals and prediction markets determine the specific policies used to achieve them). At another, a married couple gave a practical guide to IQ-screening one’s unborn children, drawn from their own experience doing the same. At the very moment that I watched Victor try and fail to emit photons from his manhood, a few doors down, a mathematician was explaining his PhD thesis on the topology of three-dimensional shapes.

Aella’s session was titled Get Hotter With Aella (Reinforcement Learning: Hot Girl Feedback). Reinforcement learning with human feedback is a technique for teaching a machine learning model to do what a human wants it to do. The model produces an output, a human rewards or penalizes it, and the cycle repeats, each round nudging the machine’s behavior closer to what the human prefers. Here, the model was Victor, and Aella, the human in the loop, was teaching him to be desired.

It didn’t work. Victor left the stage no closer to seducing his woman of choice. Round after round, the men failed. Now another man, Alex, raised his hand to face a new woman, Sam, while the room looked on. Alex’s trouble, Aella determined, was his stiffness; he was holding himself like he was bracing for a flu shot.

So she set about loosening him. “Tense your abdomen,” she told him. “I want large circles.” Alex made the circles with his arms, but Sam reported that he still felt “very stiff” to her. Everything about him, Sam added, was “feeling very robotic.”

Then Aella told Alex to touch Sam. “Reach out,” she said, “and touch Sam in a way that feels okay.” Alex smiled, a wide and sudden smile, and swung his arm up and over Sam’s shoulders in a single great swoop, planting his hand at the base of her neck, his face fixed in an awkward rictus.

“Let’s put that hand down,” Aella said. “Now let’s try it again. Step closer.” Alex stepped closer and reached for Sam a second time. Sam reported that everything about it still felt “very robotic.”

Luddite Club

“I haven’t remotely gone full-force into Luddism. Despite that, I’m here in New York at the Summer of Ludd to sing, and dance, and philosophize with the rest of them.”

Jun 30

To Phone or Not?

To Phone or Not?

I am midway down South Street, phone in hand and footsteps fast. Every time I walk down this strip of sidewalk, I think of the time during freshman year when my friends peed somewhere along this street — wherever there was some small shred of privacy to be had — their Halloween costumes not nearly warm enough in the nippy fall air.

I am walking with my phone held in front of me, eyes on the solid blue line leading the way, looking up from Apple Maps to catch the snow, the frost dusting the concrete, remembering that Penn Museum is here, I think, noticing that there is some sun out, and it is illuminating a vaguely collegiate building past the intersection. I follow my phone like it is a shepherd. I remind myself that I am going somewhere new. If you haven’t heard of Deep Springs, then (unfortunately for the Communications Committee) you are in the vast majority of Americans. The college receives only a few hundred applications per year, and once the Applications Committee is finished with its preliminary cull, only a few dozen serious applicants remain. Intense deliberation follows among the members of the Applications Committee, often stretching for multiple hours into the night, out of which emerge 13 ranked contenders and a short waitlist. At the eleventh hour, a few accepted students typically withdraw their applications in horror or indecision, and two or three students on the waitlist are tapped to take their place.

The finalized class is flown into Bishop, California and bussed an hour into the Sierra Nevada to a barren valley twice the size of Manhattan, with an abandoned prospecting settlement at its north end and a low-lying swamp, politely known as Deep Springs Lake, in the south. The college takes up a few square acres of irrigated land near the center, encircled by high, jagged mountains that rise steeply out of nothing. The valley itself is perfectly flat, smoothed by the northward passage of the glaciers at the end of the last Ice Age.

I will spare you the remaining details of this peculiar institution, since they have been told and retold with very little variation many times already — by students in The New Yorker and The London Times, as well as by professional journalists at CBS, The Economist, The Guardian, The Huffington Post, The Financial Times, Vanity Fair, Smithsonian Magazine, and, most recently, The New York Times (in addition to my own feature for The Free Press). You can pick any of these pieces to read at random, as the authors substantially agree with one another, although they respectfully space their coverage at three- or four-year intervals.

It was as the head of the Communications Committee, engaged in facilitating a visit by The Financial Times, that I first noticed this pattern. Whenever journalists visited the college, the article always seemed to turn out well. For instance, in 2009, as the college’s refusal to admit women became increasingly anomalous, Vanity Fair had written admiringly in “Cowboy Scholars” of the “academically elite college where 26 male students work a 2,500-acre cattle ranch.” After Deep Springs went co-ed in 2018, The Guardian shrugged, “[this] strange, beguiling institution in a desert valley where human purpose is debated amid coyotes and cows…will change, but perhaps not by much.”

And always you found in these articles the hatred of modernity and the handwringing about a “collapse of public trust” and a “castration of attention spans” — generously spiced, of course, with assurances that Deep Springs is “the most selective small university in the world,” that its students have an “average SAT score of 1500,” and that “many will transfer to the Ivy League.”

In the public imagination, Deep Springs was the best of both worlds. It was everything that everything else was not; it could not lose. So several months after Covid, as the outside world briefly turned its full attention towards cancel culture, I was pleased but not surprised when The Financial Times released its own report: “The U.S. College Where There Are No Culture Wars.”

I suspect that part of what makes Deep Springs so vulnerable to interpretation is the gaping hole at the college’s heart, a negative space which, elsewhere, is occupied by careerism. True, Deep Springers often find their way back toward New York City, and San Francisco, and Washington, D.C. But within the valley they are expected to keep their futures fastidiously blank. L.L. Nunn, the hydroelectric tycoon who founded Deep Springs in 1917, famously declared that his school would prepare graduates to “lead a life of service.” Yet what kind of service L.L. Nunn had in mind is not at all clear from his gnostic letters and writings, published as The Grey Book and distributed to every incoming class with a wink — as if for ambitious, well-educated Americans, blacksmithing or carpentry could be anything other than a pathetic self-indulgence.

So at Deep Springs the word “service” has been taken to imply something narrower. Namely, a humbling through work, and more specifically, ranch work: riding, herding, sorting, feeding, milking, branding, and slaughter — which is all unbelievably romantic. It is embarrassingly difficult for anyone, myself included, to shake the stubborn apprehension that they have stepped onto a movie set.

As a teenager, you want to listen to music that makes you feel free, music to play fucking loud, the kind of music your parents would demand you turn off. In short, you’re after awful-sounding music that, once illicit, becomes mystical and possessive, all yours, just yours.

Now the Story, Please

Contra Eliya Smith’s Dad Don’t Read This

Jun 9

Theater

Theater

I’m running late to catch the tail end of the thrice-extended, off-broadway run of Dad Don’t Read This, a surprise NYT Critic’s Pick by 20-something playwright Eliya Smith and director Chloe Claudel. St. Luke’s Theatre is not quite a basement, but it’s not far from it. Once I settle on a metal stool the producer sets down for me on the periphery, I clock Jesse Eisenberg with his parents in the back. Next to me, a girl with a tooth gap tells me she’s working with a director who’s worked with Nicole Kidman. So it’s a hot show.
